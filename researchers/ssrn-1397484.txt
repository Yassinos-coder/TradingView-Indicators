Optimal VWAP trading under noisy conditions
Mark L. Humphery-Jenner∗†
Journal of Banking and Finance, 2011, Vol. 35, No. 9, pp. 2319-2329
Available at http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.jbankfin.2011.01.028
Abstract
This article proposes an empirically tractable way to incorporate intra-
day noise into a VWAP trading rule. In volatile markets, news arrives un-
expectedly and rapidly. This should influence a trader’s trading decisions.
However, the literature has not incorporated such information into an al-
gorithmic trading framework. Subsequently, this paper presents a Dynamic
VWAP (DVWAP) framework that allows informed traders to utilize random
news; and thus, improve trade-execution.

1. Introduction
This paper analyzes how traders can trade on randomly arriving noise.
Volatile markets often feature unexpected or random news. The theoretical
literature indicates that such noise should influence traders’ trading decisions
(see e.g. Foster and Viswanathan, 1993, 1996). While Bialkowski, Darolles
and Le Fol (2008) suggest a way to utilize market-wide information, the
literature has not suggested a tractable trading framework in which to incor-
porate stock-specific data. Thus, existing models may not fully exploit intra-
day noise. Subsequently, this paper proposes a Dynamic VWAP (DVWAP)
framework that allows traders to utilize observable, intraday, stock-specific
information; and thus improve execution.
This paper investigates the following situation. There is an ‘large’ trader
and other market participants. The ‘ large’ trader is so called because he/she
wants to shift a large block of stock on a particular day. This can be for
liquidity reasons, or for information reasons (as per Inci, Lu and Seyhun,
2010; Kang, 2010).
The trader wants to minimize the transactions costs
involved (following Barron and Karpoff, 2004). A key transaction cost is
market impact. A way to minimize market impact is to break the large order
into smaller blocks and trade the smaller blocks throughout the day. The
other market participants trade throughout the day generating noise. This
noise may, but need not, reflect information arising throughout the day.
The large trader’s goal is optimal execution.
If the large trader acts
strategically, then optimal execution includes two key components. First,
2
the trade size literature suggests that the large trader should disguise his/her
trades by mimicking the actions of the other market participants (or noise
traders). The typical way to do this is to break the order into parcels whose
size equals the average order size for the stock (following Barclay and Warner,
1993; Chan and Fong, 2000; Cooney and Sias, 2004; Thapa and Poshakwale,
2010). Second, optimal execution should minimize transaction costs (Bauer,
Cosemans and Eichholz, 2009), operationalized by minimizing the market-
impact of a trade (Kissell, Glantz and Malamut, 2004; Kissell and Malamut,
2006).
Historical VWAP (HVWAP) algorithms attempt to achieve optimal exe-
cution. HVWAPs do this by distributing an order throughout the day so as
to mimic the historical-average intraday distribution of volume (see Madha-
van, 2002; Kakade, Kearns, Mansour and Ortiz, 2004; Bialkowski, Darolles
and Le Fol, 2008; Kearns, 2008). HVWAP does this as follows. First, over
some historical period it obtains intraday volume data.
Second, for each
given interval (‘bin’) b within the day, it calculates the proportion of total
daily volume traded in bin b. Thus, for stock i for each day t, this obtains
a set of proportions: {vi,t0, . . . , vi,tT }. The proportion in an arbitrary bin b
is vi,tb. Third, it breaks the order into smaller parcels, where the proportion
traded in bin b is vh
i,tb% of the total order.
The historical approach is problematic. The historical approach does not
consider ‘random’ news. This is problematic since random news can increase
(or decrease) the amount of volume traded in any interval b. Thus, if the
3
large trader ignores this volume spike (or lull), then he/she may imperfectly
mimic other traders. Further, if there is a volume spike (lull), then the market
impact of an order decreases (increases). Thus, if the large trader ignores the
volume spike (or lull) then he/she may trade less (more) than is optimal.
The literature has not resolved this problem. Kissell, Glantz and Malamut
(2004) and Kissell and Malamut (2006) discuss an Implementation Shortfall
(‘IS’) framework that utilizes contemporaneous market data.
The IS ap-
proach attempts to predict price movements.
It accelerates (decelerates)
trade if it predicts that the price will become less (more) favourable in the
future. However, the IS framework has two key problems.
First, the IS framework does not constrain the algorithm to distribute
trade throughout the day. This ignores the strategic possibility of waiting
for more favourable market conditions or future unexpected news releases
(as raised in Fong and Liu, 2010). This conflicts with theoretical predictions
that, in markets with high levels of unexpected news, large traders should
‘strategically’ wait for future news arrival, and this should partially reduce
the amount traded in a given trading period (see e.g. Foster and Viswanathan,
1996; Back, Cao and Willard, 2000). Second, the IS framework does not
attempt to mimic average trade sizes; and thus, may not mimic other traders
or minimize transactions costs.
Bialkowski, Darolles and Le Fol (2008) integrate some dynamic market
movements into a VWAP model. Bialkowski, Darolles and Le Fol (2008)
posit that the turnover for stock i, on day t, in time interval b (xi,tb) is
4
the historical market-average turnover in interval b over the prior month,
plus lagged intraday deviations from the market-average. They examine the
stocks in the CAC40 between the start of September 2003 and the end of
August 2004.
The Bialkowski, Darolles and Le Fol (2008) model can help predict depar-
tures from historical market-average turnover, but may not identify stock-
specific volume spikes. This is primarily because it restricts itself to analysis
of turnover. Instead, other intraday factors, such as raw-volume, spreads, or
order-book depth, may influence deviations from historical profiles. DVWAP
proposes a general model that can incorporate these variables. And, analysis
of turnover or volume is a special case. Second, it focuses on stock-specific
deviations from a common market-turnover-factor. Implicitly, this assumes
that, on average, a common market factor is a good predictor of stock-specific
turnover. Instead, a more stock-specific model may be more appropriate and
may improve the ability to detect stock-specific news.
The problems with the historical or traditional approach induce two is-
sues. First, how can the large trader utilize this random intraday information;
and, second, does utilizing the random information improve execution?
This paper proposes a Dynamic VWAP framework (DVWAP) that al-
lows VWAP algorithms to incorporate intraday stock-specific data. DVWAP
does this by both (a) basing itself on the historical intraday distribution of
trade, as in HVWAP, (b) optimizing the use of contemporaneous intraday
information so as to exploit ‘random news’. This enhances the approach in
5
Bialkowski, Darolles and Le Fol (2008) to allow traders to utilize intraday
stock-specific information; and thus, ameliorate information asymmetries.
Empirical results for 124 trading days across 130 stocks in the ASX 200
confirm that the DVWAP framework can improve execution over a purely
historical approach and has a lower tracking error (and variance thereof)
than the Bialkowski, Darolles and Le Fol (2008) approach does.
This paper contributes to the literature on optimal trading, and espe-
cially optimal algorithmic trading. Algorithmic strategies have become in-
creasingly popular (Chew, 2007), with up to 67% of investment managers
indicating that they use trading algorithims (Grossman, 2005). VWAP al-
gorithms are one of the most popular algorithms. Thus, improvements to
VWAP algorithms contribute to a growing area of financial practice. This
paper builds on the insights in Bialkowski, Darolles and Le Fol (2008) in or-
der to incorporporate stock-specific information; and thus, enhance VWAP
algorithms.
The remainder of the paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 discusses a
framework in which an informed trader might utilize ‘random news’ and
information asymmetry. Section 3 analyzes the framework. The purpose is
to show that the model can improve execution. It examines a model that
incorporates ‘unexpected’ volume data into the framework, and contrasts
this approach with that in Bialkowski, Darolles and Le Fol (2008). Section
4 concludes.

2. What is the DVWAP framework?
The goal is to improve execution. DVWAP induces better execution than
HVWAP does if it trades more (less) when the market has more (less) volume.
Thus, DVWAP has better execution than HVWAP does if it is more able to
to track the intraday dispersion of volume. Therefore, the goal of DVWAP
is to minimize tracking error. For a given stock i, the tracking error is the
difference between (a) the percentage of total daily volume traded on day
t, in bin b (va
i,tb), and (b) the percentage of the total order traded in bin
b (vd
i,tb). This induces two issues: First, what optimization problem does
DVWAP confront; and second, how does DVWAP resolve this optimization
problem.
2.1. What is the optimization problem?
DVWAP aims to reduce tracking error. The basic idea behind DVWAP
is to multiply the amount traded under a purely historical approach by a
parameter, ˆθi,tb, in order to make the algorithm track intraday volume move-
ments. This parameter derives from observable market-characteristics in the
current day. Formally, DVWAP functions by optimizing the amount traded
in each bin subject to two constraints. First, DVWAP must spread the order
throughout the day. Second, DVWAP must ensure completion of the order
and must not trade more than the total order size.

3.1. The volume example
The literature suggests that past volume information may predict future
volume spikes or lulls. The theoretical model in Foster and Viswanathan
(1993) indicates that unanticipated news affects stock volumes, and that
volumes are autocorrelated. This implies that past volume information may
help predict future volume spikes/lulls. Brooks, Patel and Su (2003) study 21
unanticipated CEO deaths, and find that the unanticipated news/information
can influence stock volume for up to an hour after the news release. Nof-
singer and Prucyk (2003) find a two hour delay between an unanticipated
14
news announcement, and a subsequent volume increase. They also find per-
sistence in volume increases. These results suggest that lagged volume may
help predict future volume movements.1
This induces three key issues. First, does contemporaneous volume con-
vey useful information; second, how would DVWAP use this information; and
third, does using the information allow DVWAP to out-perform HVWAP?
This section analyzes volume information using Reuters data for the ASX.
The sample spans 4 April 2008 to 1 October 2008 (124 trading days), with
the data from April being used solely to calculate parameters for May. The
sample contains 197 stocks in the ASX 200. The sample loses 3 stocks due to
data availability. Each day is split into 5 minute bins. Since the ASX com-
mences trade for stocks at different times (depending on the stock’s position
in the alphabet), this yields either 71 or 72 bins for each stock. In total, this
yields 1,484,980 estimates. Data analysis uses R, SAS and STATA.
The results indicate that DVWAP should incorporate lagged volume in-
formation; and that, DVWAP performs better than HVWAP does when
DVWAP incorporates contemporaneous volume information.

3.1.3. How do the changes perform on average?
The results indicate that DVWAP performs better than HVWAP does.
The trading goal is to trade more (less) if overall market volume is higher
(lower). This occurs if the algorithm tracks the intraday dispersion of trade.
Thus, DVWAP achieves better execution than HVWAP does if it reduces
the absolute average difference between (a) the proportion traded in bin b
and (b) the proportion of daily volume that trades in bin b. Thus, DVWAP
performs better than HVWAP does if both (a) it has lower tracking error on
average, and (b) it’s tracking error is lower in the majority of bins.

3.1.4. Is DVWAP’s performance robust to stock and market characteristics?
DVWAP has lower tracking error than HVWAP does on average. How-
ever, DVWAP performs better than HVWAP does only if it can outperform
HVWAP across a variety of market conditions and stock characteristics. This
paper assesses this by first sorting the data by factors that might affect
DVWAP’s performance, and then through multivariate regression analysis.
Univariate analysis indicates that DVWAP tracks actual market volume
better than HVWAP does. To assess the performance on volatile days, this
paper sorts the sample into groups based on contemporaneous volume volatil-
ity (the ‘spikiness’ of a day), historical intraday volume volatility (‘VolVar’),
and historical inter-day price volatility (‘PriceVar’). This is important due
to the reported volume-volatility relationship (see for example Xu, Chen and
Wu, 2006; Darrat, Zhong and Cheng, 2007).

Multivariate analysis confirms that DVWAP’s tracking performance is
robust to stock and market characteristics. Four key historical may influence
DVWAP’s performance ability.
First, if the stock’s intra-day volume is more volatile or if liquidity is more
unpredictable, then DVWAP is more likely to make a prediction-error, and
performance should worsen. The intra-day variance in the level of volume
between five-minute bins (‘VolVar’) proxies for this (consistent with Johnson,
2008).
Second, the average trade size may influence DVWAP’s performance. Av-
erage trade size (‘lnTradeSize’) can indicate the level of activity in a stock and
the potential for informed trade2. However, it may also indicate additional
volatility.
If average trade size represents additional activity or informed
trade, then it may provide DVWAP with more information on which to
predict volume movements; and thus, may improve DVWAP’s performance.
However, if average trade size indicates noise, then it may reduce the infor-
mativeness of past volume information.
Third, if trade in the stock is more active, then a volume-spike is less likely
to persist over time. This may harm DVWAP’s ability to trade upon pre-
dicted volume spikes. Thus, the models include ‘lnDailyTrades’: the daily-
average number of trades. Fourth, if the stock’s price is more volatile, then

3.2. DVWAP and the Bialkowski, Darolles and Le Fol (2008) approach
This section examines DVWAP and the approach in Bialkowski, Darolles

3.3. Robustness and sensitivity
This section ensures robustness that the model is robust to model speci-
fication. First, the paper re-stimates Equation (6) using different numbers of
lags. However, the results are qualitatively similar. Second, the results are
robust to bin length. Equation (6) bases itself on 5-minute bins. Results are
similar for 15 minute and 30 minute and 60 minute bins.
Third, the paper ensures that the results hold after removing outlying
bins that have unusually high volumes, which ordinarily correspond with
significant corporate announcements. DVWAP’s out-performance increases
after removing outlying bins. Fourth, Equation (6) uses three lagged differ-
ences. The paper re-estimates the model with four and five lags. The results
are not sensitive to the number of independent variables. Fifth, the paper
estimates Equation (6) using GMM to control for endogeneity that would
arise if anticipation of a volume spike influences lagged volume. The results
hold in the GMM estimation.
Sixth, the paper ensures that the results are robust to various values
of ˉθ, ˆθ, and γ. To do this, it iterates through various combinations of the
parameters.
It examines values of θ from 0.99 to 0.80, and ˉθ from 1.01
to 1.20.
It also examines values of γ from 0.75 to 0.95.
In all models,
DVWAP improves on HVWAP. If the γ value is lower, then DVWAP reverts
to HVWAP sooner; and thus, (a) the tracking increases, but (b) the standard
deviation of the tracking error decreases. If θ is lower and/or ˉθ is higher, then
(a) DVWAP’s mean improvement over HVWAP increases; that is, there is
32
lower average difference from the actual volume profile, but (b) the variance
of the tracking error increases. In all cases, DVWAP has lower tracking error
than HVWAP does.
The multivariate results in Section 3.1.4 are robust to model specification.
This paper re-estimates the models using a panel regression (with fixed stock-
date effects), and finds similar results. Further, it replaces VOLVAR with
the inter-day price-volatility (as a proxy for volatility) and replaces BaRatio
with spread (as a proxy for liquidity) and finds similar results.
The results are robust to the choice of an in-sample test. Table 3 and
Table 8 report in-sample results. All other results (unreported for brevity)
are qualitatively the same in in-sample tests. Unsurprisingly, DVWAP tends
to perform better in in-sample tests than it does in out-of-sample tests.
4. Conclusion
This paper examines the trader’s optimal trading decision if there is
random news (or noise). Random intraday information should influence a
trader’s trading behaviour (Foster and Viswanathan, 1993, 1996).
Thus,
this paper proposes a DVWAP framework that enables informed traders to
incorporate stock-specific information into a trading framework. Empirical
results indicate that this can yield superior execution to a purely historical
approach, or to the appraoches in Bialkowski, Darolles and Le Fol (2008),
when estimated using a strong empirical model for intraday volume.
This paper makes a key contribution to the algorithmic trading literature
33
by proposing a mechanism to incorporate intraday noise into an algorith-
mic trading rule. This has become especially important due to the growing
popularity of algorithmic trading.
